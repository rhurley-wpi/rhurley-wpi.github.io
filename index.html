About Me

I am a third-year PhD candidate in Civil, Environmental, and Architectural Engineering at Worcester Polytechnic Institute (WPI), where I am also affiliated with the Department of Fire Protection Engineering. Advised by Professor Shichao Liu, my research investigates the intersection of indoor air quality, wildfire smoke transport, and occupant health.


My work seeks to enhance resilience against both indoor and outdoor pollutants. By investigating laboratory-scale experiments and behavioral psychology models, I aim to understand how the built environment, material properties, and human decision-making prolong or mitigate smoke exposure. I am particularly interested in community-engaged research and disaster readiness plans.


Before my doctoral studies, I conducted research at Lafayette College focused on flash flooding and at WPI on cognitive impacts of CO2 exposure. My goal is to transition into a post-doctoral or faculty position in related environmental science and engineering fields.

Recent Projects and Activities

Internship at Aerodyne Research

In the Fall of 2024 I worked as an intern with Aerodyne Research through the NSF GEOHEALTH INTERN program. This research primarily focuses on indoor wildfire smoke transport. In this study we examined the role of the outter paint layer on drywall materials on the emission of smoke sourced VOCs. This allows for the investigation of how material properties influence both short and long-term exposure to VOCs from smoke contaminated materials. This project also used a VOCUS CI-ToF with NH4+ as the reagent ion (shown in image) and demonstrated the potential for NH4+ CIMS for smoke VOC idenification, particularly oxygenated organics. I discuss preliminary results of the study in more detail on the WPI Podcast.

Indoor Environment and Human Protective Behaviors During Wildfire Smoke Events

Both human behavior and the built environment are crucial in determining human exposure to wildfire smoke and resulting health outcomes. This research aims to account for the critical yet underrepresented role of the build environment in wildfire smoke exposure. I am working to develop a novel framework to explain wildfire smoke behavioral response that better highlights the indoor environment and indoor sensory cues.




In summer 2024, I worked with the Home Ignition Zone Project team at San Jose State University to assess community wildfire preparedness using field surveys and drone imagery. We visited homes in three California regions and analyzed how preparedness varies with landscape and built environment characteristics.
